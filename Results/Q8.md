# Q8: High-Risk Employees Who Left vs. Stayed

## Business Question

How do high-risk employees who left differ from high-risk employees who stayed?

## Results

## Methodology Note

High-risk employees were defined as employees meeting at least three of the five risk-factor conditions established in the earlier analyses: low pay, high promotion gap, overtime, low job satisfaction, and low environment satisfaction.

The analysis then separates these high-risk employees according to whether they left the company or stayed. Average job level, performance rating, job involvement, work-life balance, stock option level, tenure, tenure with current manager, and monthly income were compared between the two groups.

A supporting tenure analysis was also used to compare the minimum, median, average, and maximum years at the company between the two groups. This provides additional context for interpreting the difference in average tenure.

The analysis is descriptive and compares group characteristics. It does not establish that any individual characteristic caused an employee to leave.

The supporting analysis confirms that the tenure difference is also reflected in the median: 2.5 years for high-risk employees who left compared with 5.0 years for those who stayed. This provides additional context beyond the average alone.

## Key Finding

Among employees classified as high-risk, those who left generally had lower average job levels, job involvement, stock option levels, tenure, tenure with their current manager, and monthly income than those who stayed. Notable differences include average years at the company (3.66 vs. 6.20), average monthly income (4,311 vs. 6,124), and average years with the current manager (2.43 vs. 4.07).

The tenure difference is also present in the median values: the median tenure is 2.5 years for high-risk employees who left compared with 5.0 years for those who stayed. The supporting analysis therefore shows that the difference is not driven only by a small number of unusually long-tenured employees.

Performance rating and work-life balance show little to no difference between the two groups. Average performance rating is 3.18 among high-risk employees who left compared with 3.14 among those who stayed, while average work-life balance is identical at 2.72 for both groups.

These results suggest that, among employees already classified as high-risk, tenure, job level, job involvement, compensation, and stock options distinguish those who left from those who stayed more clearly than performance rating or work-life balance. However, these are observed group differences rather than evidence that any individual factor caused attrition.

Overall, the comparison provides a descriptive profile of high-risk employees who left versus those who stayed and can be used as a prioritization heuristic rather than a predictive or causal model.
