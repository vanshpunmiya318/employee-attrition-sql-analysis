# Q5: Promotion Stagnation and Attrition

## Business Question

How does promotion stagnation relate to employee attrition?

## Results

| Promotion Status | Stagnation Level | Total Employees | Avg Tenure | Avg Promotion Gap Ratio | Employees Left | Attrition Rate |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Zero Promotion Gap | - | 537 | 4.8 | 0.00 | 94 | 17.50% |
| Promotion Gap | Low | 297 | 9.8 | 0.16 | 31 | 10.44% |
| Promotion Gap | Medium | 296 | 10.0 | 0.40 | 36 | 12.16% |
| Promotion Gap | High | 296 | 6.3 | 0.88 | 60 | 20.27% |

## Methodology Note

Promotion stagnation was measured using the ratio of YearsSinceLastPromotion to YearsAtCompany, considering only employees with more than zero years at the company. Employees with a zero ratio were classified separately as having no promotion gap. Employees with a non-zero promotion gap were divided into three equal-sized groups using NTILE(3), based on their promotion gap ratio: Low, Medium, and High.

The promotion gap ratio represents the proportion of an employee's tenure accounted for by the time since their last promotion. A higher ratio therefore indicates a larger promotion gap relative to the employee's time at the company.

The Zero Promotion Gap group includes employees with no recorded years since their last promotion. This can include relatively recent employees as well as employees whose last promotion occurred within the previous year, and the dataset does not distinguish between these cases.

## Key Finding

Employees in the High Promotion Gap group have the highest attrition rate among employees with a non-zero promotion gap, at 20.27%. This compares with 12.16% for the Medium group and 10.44% for the Low group, showing that attrition increases as the relative promotion gap becomes larger within the non-zero gap groups.

However, the Zero Promotion Gap group has an attrition rate of 17.50%, which is higher than both the Low and Medium groups. This means the relationship is not a simple linear pattern across all four categories. The strongest observed attrition is concentrated at the High Promotion Gap level, while employees with no recorded promotion gap also show relatively high attrition.

The High Promotion Gap group also has a substantially lower average tenure (6.3 years) than the Low and Medium groups (9.8 and 10.0 years respectively). Because YearsAtCompany is the denominator of the promotion gap ratio, employees with shorter tenure can reach a high ratio with a smaller absolute promotion gap. Therefore, the High group may include relatively shorter-tenured employees with a delayed promotion rather than only long-tenured employees experiencing prolonged stagnation.

Overall, the results suggest that substantial promotion stagnation is associated with higher observed attrition, particularly among employees with a non-zero promotion gap, but the relationship should not be interpreted as a simple linear effect of promotion delay.
